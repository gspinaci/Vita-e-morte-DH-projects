ABSTRACT (ITALIANO)
La crescente proliferazione dei progetti di Digital Humanities (DH) ha generato significative sfide per la conservazione digitale, con numerosi progetti oggi non più accessibili a causa di obsolescenza tecnologica, mancato mantenimento o interruzione dei finanziamenti. Questo studio rappresenta un tentativo di disamina del ciclo di vita e della persistenza in rete dei progetti DH in ambito italiano, considerando 284 progetti realizzati dal 1998 al 2025, provenienti da fonti diverse quali atti di conferenze, piattaforme online di centri di ricerca e aggregatori di iniziative del settore. Dall'analisi è emerso che su 284 progetti, 264 hanno un URL identificato, di cui 199 sono pienamente raggiungibili e 31 accessibili solo parzialmente; 34 non risultano più raggiungibili all'indirizzo originale, ma per 9 di essi abbiamo accertato — anche tramite le risposte dei referenti — una migrazione a un nuovo indirizzo ancora attivo, lasciando circa 25 progetti effettivamente discontinuati. Esaminando questi ultimi, è stata rilevata una durata in vita media di circa 6 anni. I dati evidenziano una crescita costante del numero di nuovi progetti a partire dagli anni 2000, con un incremento significativo nell'ultimo decennio, seguito da un calo dal 2022 in poi. Per rispondere alle fragilità infrastrutturali emerse, è stata sviluppata una tassonomia del rischio volta a classificare i fattori che minacciano la sostenibilità dei progetti DH e a orientare l'adozione di contromisure preventive, con l'obiettivo finale di garantire una maggiore tutela del patrimonio culturale e accademico digitale.

ABSTRACT (ENGLISH)
The growing proliferation of Digital Humanities (DH) projects has generated significant challenges for digital preservation, with numerous projects now inaccessible due to technological obsolescence, lack of maintenance, or funding discontinuation. 
This study represents an attempt to examine the life cycle and online persistence of DH projects in the Italian context, considering 284 projects created from 1998 to 2025, drawn from various sources, including conference proceedings, online platforms of research centers, and sector initiative aggregators. The analysis revealed that out of 284 projects, 264 have an identified URL, of which 199 are fully reachable and 31 only partially accessible; 34 are no longer reachable at their original URL, but 9 of these were found — also through respondents' replies — to have migrated to a still-active new address, leaving about 25 genuinely discontinued projects. Examining the latter, the study found an average lifespan of approximately 6 years. Additionally, the data highlights a steady increase in new project launches since the early 2000s, with notable growth during the last decade, followed by a decline in net growth from 2022 onwards. To address the infrastructural vulnerabilities identified, a risk taxonomy was developed to classify the factors threatening the sustainability of DH projects and to guide the adoption of preventive countermeasures, with the ultimate goal of ensuring greater protection of digital cultural and academic heritage. 

Keywords: DH projects; technological obsolescence; digital preservation; digital persistence; AIUCD2025.
1. INTRODUCTION
The field of Digital Humanities (DH) is an interdisciplinary domain in constant evolution, with projects often tied to temporary funding and rapidly changing technologies. While considerable attention has been devoted to the creation and dissemination of DH projects, comparatively less effort has been directed towards understanding their persistence over time. The longevity of a DH project reflects not only technical aspects but also broader issues related to the sustainability of research practices and digital scholarly outputs. The loss of access to such projects may entail the disappearance of data and knowledge, reducing the long-term impact of the time, expertise, and resources invested in their creation and potentially hindering progress in specific research domains. 
A central challenge in studying this phenomenon lies in defining what constitutes a DH project.  The field encompasses a broad spectrum of activities, ranging from databases and software applications to large-scale digital infrastructures, digital editions, online archives, and research environments. Establishing a universally accepted definition remains difficult, particularly when attempting large-scale empirical analyses. For the purposes of this study, we adopt an operational definition based on public online visibility. We consider DH projects those initiatives that produce a publicly accessible digital output identifiable through a dedicated web presence, such as a project website, digital edition, online archive, database, or platform. This choice introduces important limitations. A project cannot be reduced to its website, nor does the absence of a public URL necessarily imply the absence of scholarly activity. Many research initiatives may continue to exist through local infrastructures, internal repositories, unpublished datasets, software components, or institutional environments that are not publicly accessible. Nevertheless, a web presence remains one of the most visible manifestations of a DH project and often constitutes the primary point of access for the wider public. Furthermore, the existence of a stable URL provides a measurable and reproducible criterion for conducting longitudinal analyses at scale. Rather than claiming to represent the entirety of DH activity, this study focuses on the subset of Italian DH projects whose outputs are publicly accessible online and therefore observable through computational and manual inspection methods.
Despite growing discussions on sustainability and long-term preservation within Digital Humanities, no systematic investigation has yet examined the availability and persistence of DH projects at a national scale in the Italian context. As a result, there is still limited evidence regarding how many projects remain accessible over time, how long they tend to remain available, and which factors contribute either to their continued accessibility or to their eventual disappearance. 
To address this gap, we compiled a dataset of Italian DH projects collected from three main categories of sources: project aggregators, conference proceedings, and the websites of research centres and institutions active in the field. The resulting dataset was subsequently enriched through semi-automated and manual verification procedures aimed at assessing the accessibility and current status of each project. For the purposes of this study, project availability is evaluated through the accessibility of their publicly visible digital outputs and associated web resources. Since project histories are rarely documented in a systematic way, the first and last verifiable online traces of a project are used as proxies for estimating its period of public availability. 
Beyond the quantitative analysis of project availability, this study also investigates the factors associated with project sustainability. To complement the observational data, we conducted two anonymous qualitative surveys involving approximately thirty project representatives, including coordinators of both currently accessible and no-longer-accessible projects. The surveys explored aspects such as maintenance practices, funding models, institutional support, technological choices, and perceived causes of success or failure. This mixed-methods approach allows us to move beyond the simple identification of accessible and inaccessible resources and to investigate the organizational, technical, and infrastructural conditions that influence project persistence over time. Furthermore, the combination of quantitative observations and qualitative evidence supports the development of a taxonomy designed to describe the different states, accessibility conditions, preservation scenarios, and risk factors characterizing the lifecycle of DH projects. 
The study is guided by the following research questions:
RQ1: What is the current state of availability and accessibility of publicly visible DH projects in Italy, and what can be inferred about their persistence over time? 
RQ2: Which technical, organizational, and institutional factors are associated with the long-term sustainability of DH projects?
RQ3: Which factors are most frequently associated with the degradation, discontinuation, or loss of accessibility of DH projects?
RQ4: How can the resulting evidence be formalized through taxonomies and computational workflows to support future monitoring and sustainability assessment activities?
By combining large-scale project mapping, accessibility assessment, and qualitative evidence gathered directly from project stakeholders, this research aims to provide an initial framework for studying the sustainability of DH initiatives. In addition, it proposes a taxonomy of project sustainability conditions that captures the diverse trajectories observed during the study, including project transformations, accessibility patterns, preservation outcomes, and causes of discontinuation. The taxonomy is intended both as an analytical framework for interpreting project lifecycles and as a foundation for future semantic modelling efforts, including potential alignment with CIDOC-CRM and related ontologies for digital preservation and provenance. Beyond offering a snapshot of the current Italian landscape, the study seeks to establish methodological foundations that can be expanded to broader national and international contexts and support future efforts toward automated monitoring, semantic modelling, and long-term preservation of Digital Humanities projects. 
2. RELATED WORKS
While extensive research exists on the disappearance of digital resources, our work focuses specifically on DH projects, an area where studies remain limited and primarily concern long-term sustainability methods. DH projects differ from general digital resources due to their purpose of preserving cultural and humanistic heritage, often acting as an alternative, complement, or enhancement to traditional physical preservation practices. If DH projects aim to achieve this goal of long-term preservation, their lifespan on the web should ideally be unlimited. However, this is often not the case, as many projects face challenges such as technological obsolescence, lack of active maintenance, and eventual inaccessibility.
Previous studies on the longevity and decline of DH projects have focused mainly on the North American context. For instance, VandeCreek (2022) highlights the importance of institutional support in mitigating project decline, noting that academic projects are 4.15 times more likely to sustain their digital resources than non-academic ones. Furthermore, it also reveals that 68% of early funded projects (1996-2003) remain accessible online. 
The longevity of DH projects has also been analyzed by Shelf Life: Identifying the Abandonment of Online Digital Humanities Projects (Meneses and Furuta 2019). Their study, based on an analysis of URLs from conference abstracts within the Alliance of Digital Humanities Organizations, concludes that the average lifespan of unmaintained online DH initiatives is approximately five years before disappearance. A broader and methodologically influential perspective on the decay of online resources comes from the study of “reference rot”. Klein et al. (2014) analysed over one million web references contained in roughly 3.5 million scholarly articles published between 1997 and 2012, finding that approximately one in five articles suffers from reference rot. Crucially, they distinguish two distinct phenomena: “link rot”, in which a referenced resource ceases to be available altogether, and content drift, in which the resource remains reachable but its content has changed to the point of no longer being representative of what was originally cited. This distinction is directly relevant to the present study: a project URL returning a successful HTTP response does not guarantee that the corresponding DH resource is still functional or even present, which is precisely why our workflow complements automated status-code checks with manual inspection and Wayback Machine analysis. The combination of link rot and content drift also helps to frame the cases we classify as showcase pages or partially accessible projects, where the web presence persists while the scholarly output has effectively degraded. However, the methodology used in this work has its limitations: it includes URLs that are irrelevant to the analysis, such as those linked to external tools or unrelated projects, and excludes projects not presented at conferences. Furthermore, the exclusive reliance on HTTP responses provides only a partial understanding of a project's status, failing to capture complex issues such as functional failures of seemingly active websites.
Moreover, the inability to preserve digital humanities beyond a few years has been investigated further in the Sustainability of digital humanities projects as a publication and documentation challenge (Edmond and Morselli 2020). They identify evolving needs, limited resources, inadequate sustainability planning, and "orphaning" as primary causes of project discontinuation. Using the Bamboo Project (Dombrowski 2014) as a case study, they illustrate how late attempts at sustainability following the end of funding could not save the initiative. Key factors in the project's failure included insufficient funding, a lack of planned interoperability, and a lack of strategic planning and a cohesive vision among stakeholders. Broadening the discussion, technical neglect and the discontinuation of software, such as Adobe Flash Player, a phenomenon often referred to as “bit rot”, pose significant threats to the sustainability of DH projects (VandeCreek 2022). Beyond diagnosing the causes of project decline, a growing body of work proposes concrete organizational and technical responses. At an institutional scale, Smithies et al. (2019) describe the archiving and sustainability programme undertaken by King's Digital Lab (King's College London) to manage a portfolio of roughly one hundred legacy DH projects accumulated over twenty years, treating long-term maintenance as a research-software-engineering problem rather than an afterthought. From a complementary, design-oriented angle, the Endings Project at the University of Victoria argues that projects should be conceived from the outset to "end gracefully", advocating the production of static outputs with minimal server dependencies as the most sustainable archival form (Holmes et al. 2023). Notably, the Endings team based part of its recommendations on a survey of 127 DH projects examining the various ways in which such initiatives reach their end, a sample comparable in scale to the one analysed here. Similar concerns have recently been raised in the Italian context. Cucurullo et al. (2025) discuss the risks posed by obsolete technologies and file formats in the preservation of dialectal resources, using the Atlante Lessicale Toscano as a case study and proposing a FAIRification strategy for an existing resource. 
To our knowledge, this study represents the first effort to propose a general examination of the sustainability and lifespan of DH projects in Italy, addressing this critical gap in the literature.
3. METHODOLOGY
This study adopts a mixed-methods approach combining automated data collection, manual validation, web archival analysis, and qualitative investigation. The methodology was designed to address both the observable dimensions of DH project availability and the organizational factors influencing their long-term sustainability. The following section describes the different stages of the methodological workflow, from data collection and validation to project status assessment and qualitative inquiry (Fig. 1).

3.1 SOURCE SELECTION AND DATASET CONSTRUCTION 
To analyze the lifecycle of Italian DH projects, we defined geographical and thematic boundaries of what is considered part of this group (see the Introduction). We deemed these projects’ URLs relevant for this analysis so that we can assess the accessibility status of projects based on status codes and the Internet Archive’s Wayback Machine snapshot dates of HTTP requests against their URLs. Following the operational definition introduced above, project URLs were used as the primary observable indicator of a project's public availability and accessibility over time. Internet Archive snapshots are estimates, not a comprehensively reliable point in time describing the health of a website. Currently, the literature lacks an aggregator of projects with the characteristics above. Hence, we adopted the methodology presented in Spinaci et al. (2022) in an attempt to create such an aggregator in the form of a dataset, integrating manually assessed external sources.
With these premises, the experiment started by detecting the typologies of sources where information about projects could be found: aggregators, proceedings, and research center websites. The selected sources were chosen because they represent complementary perspectives on the Italian DH ecosystem. Project aggregators provide broad coverage across institutions and disciplines; conference proceedings capture projects at the moment of scholarly dissemination and often document initiatives that are not indexed elsewhere; research centre websites offer direct evidence of projects formally maintained by institutions active in the field. For the aggregators, we scraped DH Awards, a website where the DH community can nominate resources, and an international committee votes for the nominated based on categories. Each source underwent manual validation and normalization. Entries corresponding to local software tools, international projects outside the scope of this study, duplicated resources or records lacking sufficient evidence of an identifiable DH project were excluded following the criteria defined in the Introduction. The other three project aggregators are the KNOT Catalogue, an archive containing digital scholarly resources among projects and objects, the dataset "Classification of web-based Digital Humanities projects" (Battisti 2024) and  the “Catalogue of Digital Editions” (Franzini 2012) from where we extracted the ones relevant to the experiment. For the proceedings, we manually assessed the AIUCD Conference Proceedings dated 2013, 2014, and those ranging from 2016 to 2024, looking for articles describing or referencing projects, preferably with URLs. Another set of conference proceedings is the "Information and Research Science Connecting to Digital and Library Science", IRCDL, of which we manually assessed the period from 2005 to 2019. The last typology container considered is the research center websites. We started by collecting AIUCD-affiliated research centers and integrating the list with the missing ones of our knowledge. We scraped each of their websites to get a list of their published projects. Although their coverage is far from complete, these sources have been selected based on their availability and relevance to provide a comprehensive overview of DH projects in the Italian landscape.
3.2 DATA CLEANING
During the manual assessment, each project was checked to agree to the conformity parameters defined in the previous sections. Each project URL was individually inspected by accessing the corresponding web resource through a browser rather than relying exclusively on HTTP response codes. This procedure was necessary because websites may return successful responses while presenting unavailable content, broken functionalities, authentication barriers, incomplete migrations, or other issues not detectable through automated checks alone. During the inspection process, we recorded: accessibility of the homepage; availability of project contents; availability of search interfaces and interactive features; presence of error messages or deprecated technologies; evidence of recent maintenance or updates. This step's output is a list of projects per source, each with its corresponding URL. The second step is to merge the project lists. We decided to review the list’s content before applying computational techniques to disambiguate and eliminate the duplicates. We noticed some projects repeated with different URLs, a behavior explainable by their physiological evolution over time. These cases were handled manually, keeping only the row with an existing website, i.e., with a resolving URL. After this preliminary review, we could automatically remove rows with duplicate URLs. The final result is a list containing 284 Italian Digital Humanities projects, of which 2.8% from Catalogue of Digital Editions, 4.2% come from the DH awards website, 12.0% from IRCDL proceedings, 19.7% from AIUCD proceedings, 23.6% from the KNOT Catalogue and 37.7% from Italian DH institutions.
3.3 PROJECT STATUS CLASSIFICATION AND TEMPORAL ANALYSIS
The next step is to enrich the data with additional information. We encountered cases where a showcase website or a landing page existed (quite common when public grants fund projects), while, to the best of our knowledge, we could not find the project URL. This has been noted for 20 projects. For the remaining 264 projects, we controlled the status code of HTTP requests against their URL and analyzed their coverage over time by accessing the Wayback Machine APIs through a custom script. This script retrieves snapshots of each website, which, when filtered based on 2xx status codes, can provide an estimate of the period during which the website was or is available. Wayback Machine’s crawlers do not give the exact date when a website appeared or disappeared from the web, but they offer a temporal guide. We can infer that a website was published before the first snapshot timestamp. Regarding the last snapshot, if a website is not resolved, we can infer that the website went offline after the last snapshot timestamp. In addition to the script, we manually inspected the Wayback Machine snapshots of unavailable projects to perform a qualitative check and determine whether the projects existed by analyzing the website’s content. For each project, we evaluated whether it had a website, if it was accessible, whether its content was available, and if we encountered an outdated or inactive URL. When inactive or unresolved URLs were encountered, additional manual investigation was performed to determine whether the project had been migrated rather than discontinued. This process included searching institutional websites, project documentation, conference papers, archived versions of the websites through the Wayback Machine, search engine queries, and references contained in related publications. Whenever a new domain was identified, we verified whether the original URL implemented a proper redirect and recorded the migration status accordingly. Fig. 2 visually describes the process.

3.4 QUALITATIVE SURVEY
To complement the observational dataset, we conducted a qualitative survey aimed at collecting direct evidence on the sustainability practices, maintenance dynamics, and discontinuation factors of Italian DH projects. The survey phase was designed to support a deeper interpretation of the accessibility data by incorporating the perspectives of project coordinators and institutional representatives. The first sample consisted of 31 projects that were no longer active as of April 2026. These projects were selected from the broader set of unavailable initiatives identified during the quantitative analysis, which included 46 inactive projects overall. This sample was selected heterogeneously in order to include projects with different objectives, technological platforms, institutional affiliations, geographic distribution across Italy, and varying levels of organizational scale and complexity. An anonymous online questionnaire was sent to the identified project referents, asking about the causes of discontinuation, maintenance challenges, funding history, technological issues, institutional support, preservation strategies, and the perceived critical factors that affected the project over time. A second survey targeted 31 currently active projects to balance the number of inactive projects included in the previous questionnaire. These projects were selected according to the same criteria presented above. The questionnaire was adapted to focus on sustainability practices, governance models, maintenance workflows, funding continuity, technological choices, and strategies adopted to ensure long-term accessibility. Both surveys were conducted anonymously to encourage frank responses and reduce the risk of institutional or personal bias. The collected information does not aim to provide statistically representative conclusions about the entire Italian DH ecosystem; rather, it serves as qualitative evidence capable of contextualizing the quantitative findings and revealing the organizational, technical, and infrastructural realities that are often not visible through web-based observation alone. This qualitative component enabled the study to move beyond merely identifying accessible and inaccessible projects and to investigate the underlying conditions, experiences, and decision-making processes that influence the long-term sustainability of DH initiatives.
3.5 TAXONOMY DEVELOPMENT 
Beyond the collection and assessment of project accessibility data, the study aimed to develop a conceptual framework capable of describing the different sustainability conditions observed across the Italian DH landscape. During the manual validation process, the temporal analysis of project websites, and the qualitative investigation conducted through the surveys, a wide variety of recurring situations emerged that could not be adequately represented through a simple distinction between active and inactive. 
To systematically capture these observations, a taxonomy was developed alongside the empirical analysis. The taxonomy emerged inductively from the cases encountered during the study and surveys and was progressively refined as new patterns were identified. The objective was to create a structured vocabulary capable of describing the lifecycle of DH projects and the conditions affecting their long-term sustainability. The formalization work draws inspiration from the Taxonomy of Digital Research Activities in the Humanities (TaDiRAH), which has demonstrated the value of community-driven taxonomic frameworks for describing and organizing Digital Humanities activities. While TaDiRAH focuses primarily on research methods and activities, the taxonomy proposed in this study addresses the different domains of a project’s lifecycle evolution. Our taxonomy was organized around four complementary dimensions: project status, accessibility, data preservation, and causes of project termination or risk factors. These dimensions were intentionally designed to be independent and combinable. For example, a project may be classified as archived while remaining fully accessible; a migrated project may simultaneously exhibit partial data preservation. The framework therefore, captures sustainability as a multidimensional phenomenon rather than a binary state. The resulting taxonomy serves both as an analytical instrument for interpreting the dataset and as a reusable framework for future monitoring activities. Although developed in the context of Italian DH projects, the categories were defined at a level of abstraction intended to facilitate reuse and adaptation in broader Digital Humanities contexts. Furthermore, the taxonomy was conceived with future semantic formalization in mind. The conceptual structure has been designed to support its evolution into an ontology aligned with existing cultural heritage and digital preservation standards, particularly the CIDOC-CRM family of ontologies and extensions such as CRMdig. This future development would enable interoperability with existing heritage knowledge graphs, support machine-readable sustainability assessments, and facilitate the integration of project lifecycle information within broader digital ecosystem models.
3.6 METHODOLOGICAL LIMITATIONS
The combination of automated data collection, manual validation, temporal reconstruction through web archives, and qualitative investigation allowed us to construct a dataset that captures not only the existence of Italian DH projects but also their accessibility status and observable evolution over time. Nevertheless, several methodological limitations must be acknowledged.
A first limitation concerns the operational definition of a DH project adopted in this study. As said before, in order to ensure consistency and reproducibility, the analysis focuses on projects that produce a publicly accessible digital output identifiable through a dedicated web presence. This criterion enables systematic observation and comparability across cases, but inevitably excludes forms of DH activity that are not primarily web-exposed, such as projects hosted in institutional repositories, embedded in research infrastructures, or disseminated through software components or non-public datasets. The dataset therefore, reflects the publicly visible segment of the Italian DH ecosystem rather than its full empirical extension. A second limitation relates to data coverage. Although aggregators, conference proceedings, and research centre websites were selected for their relevance and established role within the Italian DH community, the absence of a comprehensive national registry means that some projects may not have been captured, in particular, the first DH projects (90s-2000s). This constraint is intrinsic to the current fragmentation of DH documentation rather than a limitation of the extraction procedure itself. Additional challenges emerge in the identification and classification of projects. In some cases, available documentation provided incomplete information regarding institutional affiliation, funding structures, collaborations, or outputs. These ambiguities required interpretative decisions during manual validation. To mitigate potential inconsistencies, classification was conducted using conservative and uniform criteria, although a residual degree of uncertainty remains. Finally, the assessment of project accessibility presents inherent methodological constraints. Automated checks based on HTTP status codes provide a scalable proxy for availability, but do not fully account for functional accessibility or user experience. Manual verification improves accuracy but cannot eliminate the effects of temporal volatility in web infrastructures or entirely remove subjective interpretation in borderline cases. Moreover, manual detection of projects from proceedings is error-prone for two reasons: the absence of standardized conventions for locating project references (whether in footnotes or body text) and the substantial volume of proceedings that requires manual analysis. 
Despite these limitations, the resulting dataset offers a reproducible and extensible foundation for analysing the sustainability of DH projects.
4. RESULTS
This section reports the results of the quantitative and qualitative analyses and presents an attempt to create a representative taxonomy of failures.
4.1 QUANTITATIVE RESULTS
As of 28th May 2026 (the date cutoff that defines this analysis), this preliminary study identified 284 Digital Humanities projects (available online) from Italy, spanning the years 1998–2025. Among these, 20 do not yet have a project website, although a platform had previously been announced. Of the remaining 264, 199 have a fully functioning website and 31 are partially inaccessible; 34 are no longer reachable at their original URL. Of these 34, 9 were found to have migrated to a still-active address (without a redirect from the original URL), leaving about 25 genuine discontinuations. In total, 11 projects had migrated to a new domain without implementing a redirect (see §4.2).

The resulting data outline an evolution of DH projects over time, as illustrated in (Fig. 3, A). The trend indicates a steady increase, with negligible fluctuations, in new online project launches per year starting from the early 2000s, with a significant surge in activity during the last decade. This increase is perhaps also correlated with the broader institutionalization of DH in Italy, including the establishment of new dedicated research centers and funding opportunities (Sprugnoli et al. 2019). The surge in new projects also correlates with global trends in the DH field, as evidenced by Google Trends data reflecting increasing interest and activity in the domain nationally.
The Growth Rate (Fig. 3, B), normalized by the number of cumulative projects, further contextualizes this trend. The chart reflects the annual difference between new and unavailable projects, emphasizing the consistent growth of the DH landscape. Despite fluctuations, the positive net difference observed in most years indicates a general expansion of the field, especially during the period from 2016 to 2022, which saw the most substantial increase in the number of active projects in absolute terms. However, the declining growth from 2023 onwards might suggest an emerging gap between project launches and project discontinuations, possibly reflecting the challenges of maintaining long-term sustainability amidst growing complexity or resource constraints, or that after more than 20 years, a critical mass of projects is being reached whose natural life cycle is coming to an end. The project’s timespans can also be tied to natural cycles or the conclusion of funding for upcoming releases tied to current national initiatives (e.g., PNRR/PRIN), potentially leading to a new increase in activity from 2026. In either case, for discontinued projects, the average lifespan was calculated as the period between the first and last snapshots recorded by the Wayback Machine.
Preliminary findings suggest that, among the currently unavailable projects, the average lifespan was relatively short, with the majority ceasing activity within 6 years of launch. This lifespan is broadly consistent with the approximately five-year lifespan reported for unmaintained DH initiatives in the North American context (Meneses and Furuta 2019). However, this estimate should be interpreted with caution because the current number of unavailable projects is relatively small. As previous investigation observed (Klein et al. 2014), the link rot (the outright disappearance of a resource) must be distinguished from cases in which the resource persists but has moved or changed. The evidence from this investigation of projects migrated without a redirect indicates that part of what the liveness check records as loss reflects link rot at the original URL rather than the genuine discontinuation of the project. The reported lifespan should therefore be read as an upper bound on disappearance rather than a definitive measure of it.
4.2 QUALITATIVE RESULTS
To complement the observational data, we examined responses to the two anonymous surveys and follow-up replies received via e-mail. Given the limited size of the two samples, the following results should be read as indicative. Nevertheless, they outline a consistent set of patterns that help interpret the quantitative trends described above. 
The two groups of active (n=14) and discontinued (n=12) projects are structurally similar in the typology of their outputs (predominantly databases, archives, and digital editions), in team size (mostly two to five people), and in their university affiliations. The clearest divergence relates to sustainability practices. Projects that are still accessible score higher than the others on all four sustainability items surveyed, but the gap is much higher for the existence of technical documentation. The presence of a figure formally responsible for long-term maintenance follows the same pattern, with 57% of active projects and 33% of discontinued ones reporting it. Initial sustainability planning, by contrast, remains the weakest item for both groups, suggesting that planning for persistence is a shared structural weakness of the field rather than a discriminating trait between survival and discontinuation.
A particularly relevant pattern, shown in Fig. 4, concerns the misalignment between the causes of discontinuation anticipated by active projects and those actually reported by discontinued ones. Because the two groups differ in size and because respondents could select more than one factor, the values reported here are normalized. When asked which factors they perceive as most threatening, respondents of active projects concentrated their concern on technological obsolescence (32% of their selections) and the abandonment of key personnel, or “orphaning” (28.5%), placing the end of funding only third (21.5%). Discontinued projects, however, reported an almost inverse distribution: the end of funding without a continuation plan accounted for the largest share of their responses (42.9%), followed by institutional restructuring or lack of institutional support (28.6%), while technological obsolescence and orphaning represented a much smaller portion (14.3% each). This indicates that, within this sample, projects tend to disappear more often for economic and institutional reasons than for technical ones, even though those still active perceive the technical threat as predominant.

A recurring element in the e-mail responses concerned project migration. Several projects flagged as unavailable because their URLs were offline were actually migrated. Representatives who replied via email pointed us to a new address where the resource is still hosted, indicating that the projects had not ceased to exist. In most of these cases, however, no redirect has been implemented from the original URL, so that the resource remains effectively unreachable for any user relying on the previously published or indexed address. In total, 11 projects had migrated to a new domain without implementing a redirect; 9 of them had initially been recorded as no longer reachable and were reclassified as available once the migration was confirmed. This practice suggests that the loss of accessibility can also be caused by the absence of redirect strategies rather than by a proper discontinuation of the project itself.
Finally, the surveys reveal conditions shared across both groups that point to systemic fragilities of the sector. The requirement for a sustainability or data management plan by funding bodies is almost entirely absent: it was reported by only 17% of discontinued projects and by none of the active projects, with the remaining respondents either declaring its absence or being unaware of any such requirement. Warning signs also appear to be poorly recognized, as 83% of discontinued projects declared that no premonitory signals had been observed; yet, when such signals were reported, they concerned departing personnel and expiring funding, precisely the factors that the same respondents identified as the actual causes of discontinuation. Notably, 29% of currently active projects already recognize analogous signals, and the perceived probability of facing future maintenance problems among active projects is relatively high (average 3.50 out of 5), with 21% reporting that they have already experienced episodes of inaccessibility due to causes such as cyberattacks, storage limitations, the unavailability of institutional servers, or precarious staffing.
4.3 PROPOSED TAXONOMY
The taxonomy was designed to describe the observable lifecycle, accessibility, preservation status, and termination conditions of Digital Humanities projects. Rather than modelling projects through a single linear lifecycle, the taxonomy adopts a multidimensional approach that captures four complementary dimensions: Project Status, Cause of Ending, Accessibility, and Data Preservation (Appendix A).
DH Project:
Project Status: Describes the current observable condition of a project.
Accessibility: Describes the degree to which project resources are accessible to users.
Data Preservation: Describes the survival status of project data independently from project accessibility.
Causes of Ending: Describes the primary factor contributing to project termination.
Project Status:
Active: The project remains operational and maintained.

An important characteristic of the proposed framework is the independence of its dimensions. Categories belonging to different dimensions can be combined to describe complex sustainability scenarios. This multidimensional structure allows the taxonomy to capture a broad range of sustainability conditions that would remain invisible under binary classifications. The taxonomy should therefore be understood as a descriptive framework rather than an evaluative model. Its purpose is not to assign sustainability scores, but to provide a common vocabulary for documenting and monitoring the lifecycle of DH projects. Beyond its analytical role, the taxonomy may also serve as a reflective framework for project planning and management. By making explicit the different dimensions involved in project sustainability, the framework can help project teams identify critical aspects that are often overlooked during the design and development phases. Rather than prescribing best practices, the taxonomy aims to support awareness and informed decision-making by highlighting factors that may influence the long-term accessibility and persistence of Digital Humanities projects. 
5. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE DEVELOPMENTS
This study offers the first systematic assessment of the lifecycle and online persistence of Digital Humanities projects in the Italian context, combining large-scale project mapping, automated accessibility checks, web archival analysis, and qualitative surveys to examine a landscape previously unexplored at the national scale. 
The picture highlights a field that has expanded greatly over the past two decades, yet shows clear fragilities: a portion of the projects is only partially accessible, no longer reachable, or was never publicly released, and the working life of those who disappear tends to be short. However, on closer inspection, much of the apparent loss reflects link rot of originally published addresses rather than the genuine end of these initiatives. Questionnaires submitted to representatives of discontinued and active projects help to explain this fragility. Survival appears to depend less on the technical dimension than on how a project is sustained: the examined projects are structurally alike in their outputs, teams, and institutional settings, and what most clearly distinguishes the survivors are practices such as technical documentation and a designated responsibility for long-term maintenance, although the small size of the samples means these patterns should be read as indicative rather than conclusive. Importantly, active projects identify technological obsolescence as their main concern, whereas discontinued ones trace their end to the exhaustion of funding and a lack of institutional support. This divergence is compounded by a near-total absence of sustainability or data management plans required by funding bodies, reported by only a minority of discontinued projects and by none of the active ones. Read together, the findings reframe DH sustainability less as a matter of preserving technology than of sustaining the conditions that keep projects alive. 
Project’s limitations lie in the way data is aggregated and cleaned, as well as in the limited subset of DH projects analyzed. Due to the current lack of an aggregator that comprehensively tracks all past and ongoing DH projects, manual assessment was necessary. However, this approach is not a deterministic method for categorizing projects. Further standardized distinctions within DH project aggregators, such as the efforts undertaken by the KNOT Catalogue and ATLAS, could help eliminate the need for manual evaluation and reduce ambiguities during dataset cleaning. While the manual process adopted in this study ensured a high level of accuracy through direct human verification and implicitly assessed the FAIR principle of Findability (Tóth-Czifra 2020), it was also time-consuming and susceptible to human error, highlighting the need for more scalable approaches.
Future work will explore the semi-automatic identification and monitoring of DH projects from conference proceedings. As demonstrated by this study, the manual assessment of sources such as AIUCD and IRCDL proceedings proved time-intensive and difficult to scale. A possible direction involves the use of document parsing tools such as GROBID to extract bibliographic metadata and project-related URLs from conference proceedings in PDF format, combined with web crawling techniques to collect information about the referenced resources. This information could then be analyzed through LLM-based disambiguation methods to determine whether a URL corresponds to a DH project and to enrich the dataset with additional metadata. Such an approach would facilitate the expansion of the dataset while reducing the manual effort required for project identification and verification. Preliminary experiments with this pipeline have shown promising results, suggesting that the proposed approach is a viable direction for future investigation.  Moreover, expanding the research internationally would enable cross-national comparisons and might uncover region-specific factors affecting DH project longevity. By addressing these areas, future studies can contribute to ensuring the sustainability and accessibility of DH projects, preserving their scholarly and cultural impact for the long term.
